November 2026 edition

Make a household plan and prepare for outages, unsafe water, injuries, fires and natural hazards.

In immediate danger?
Follow instructions from local authorities. Call your local emergency number as soon as it is safe to do so.

1. Household plan and three-day kit

Write down your emergency number, official alert channels, an out-of-area contact and two meeting places: one near home and one outside the neighbourhood. Work out how children will be collected and how anyone who needs accessible transport, backup power or daily care will get help. Keep paper copies and practise the plan.

Store at least three days of essentials at home. Plan for roughly four litres of water per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene, with extra for pets, heat and medical needs. Stock shelf-stable food, a manual can opener, a torch, radio, spare batteries, power bank, hygiene supplies and a first-aid kit.

Pack a bag you can carry with water, snacks, medication, essential documents, cash, warm layers and a phone charger. Ask a pharmacist about extra medicine and storage. Check supplies and batteries twice a year.

2. Safe water and sanitation

Check the exact water advisory. If officials say not to drink or use tap water, follow the limits they give and use a safe supply instead. Use safe water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, ice and infant formula.

For a boil-water advisory, use sealed bottled water or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Above about 2,000 metres, boil it for three minutes. Let it cool in a clean, covered container. Boiling, household filters and disinfectants cannot make water contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive material safe.

Wash your hands with soap and safe water, especially after using the toilet and before handling food. If water and soap are unavailable, use hand sanitiser containing at least 60% alcohol.

3. First aid while waiting for help

Check for dangers such as traffic, fire and live wires before approaching. For a life-threatening emergency, call the local emergency number, give your exact location and follow the dispatcher’s instructions. If someone is unresponsive and is not breathing normally or is only gasping, start CPR and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) as soon as it is available. Follow its prompts.

For heavy external bleeding, press firmly and continuously on the wound with gauze or a clean cloth. If an object is embedded, leave it in place and press around it.

Cool a heat burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not use ice or pull away anything stuck to the burn. Seek urgent help for large, deep, electrical or chemical burns. Learn first aid and CPR in a practical course before an emergency.

4. Power outages and food safety

Use a torch or battery lantern. Run generators only outdoors at least six metres from doors, windows and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from buildings. Never use a gas oven, grill or camping stove indoors for heat. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get outside into fresh air and call emergency services.

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. An unopened fridge keeps perishables cold for about four hours. A closed full freezer lasts about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24 hours. After four hours without power or another cold source, discard refrigerated perishables. Never taste food to check its safety.

If you depend on powered medical equipment or refrigerated medicine, ask your provider in advance about safe backup power, storage and when to relocate.

5. Home fires and wildfires

If a smoke alarm sounds in your home or you see fire there, get out immediately. Stay low beneath smoke, use stairs and close doors behind you. Leave hot doors closed and use another exit. Meet outside and call the fire service from safety. Tell responders if anyone is missing. Stay out.

If you cannot leave, shut the door between you and the fire, block gaps around it with cloth if available, call for help and signal at a window. Practise two ways out of each room where possible.

For a wildfire, leave promptly when ordered. If officials advise sheltering from smoke, shut windows and doors and use filtered recirculated air if possible. If your home gets too hot or smoky, seek a designated cleaner-air shelter if safe.

6. Floods and severe storms

When water starts to rise, move to higher ground. Keep out of floodwater on foot or in a vehicle. It can conceal damaged roads, sharp debris and live wires. If trapped in a building, move to the highest safe level and call for rescue. Avoid a closed attic with no usable exit.

If a tropical storm threatens, follow evacuation orders early, especially in coastal flood zones. For a tornado, use a designated shelter or a small windowless room on the lowest safe level of a sturdy building. Protect your head and neck. If flooding also threatens, avoid basements and follow local instructions.

When you hear thunder, go into a substantial building or a fully enclosed vehicle with a hard metal roof. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back out.

7. Earthquakes and tsunamis

Indoors during shaking, get down on your hands and knees, take cover under a sturdy table and hold on. If no cover is nearby, get low beside an interior wall and shield your head and neck. If you use a wheelchair, lock its wheels and cover your head and neck. Stay inside while shaking continues.

If outdoors, move away from buildings, trees and power lines. Once shaking stops, watch for aftershocks and keep away from damaged buildings.

Near the coast, strong or prolonged shaking, an abrupt change in sea level or a loud roar from the sea can signal a tsunami. As soon as you can move safely, head uphill or inland without waiting for an alert. Return only when officials say it is safe.

8. Extreme heat and cold

In extreme heat, take breaks in a cool place, drink water regularly and avoid heavy activity at the hottest time. Check on people who live alone or need help. Never leave a person or pet alone in a parked vehicle.

Confusion, a seizure or loss of consciousness in the heat may mean heatstroke. Call emergency services and begin cooling immediately. Move the person to shade, remove outer layers and cool the skin with wet cloths or cool water. Give the person nothing to drink.

In severe cold, shelter from wind and wear dry layers. Shivering with confusion, slurred speech or drowsiness can signal hypothermia. Call emergency services. Handle the person gently, move them to warmth, remove wet clothes and wrap their body in dry layers.

9. Getting lost outdoors

Before leaving, check weather and trail conditions. Tell someone your route and when to call for help if you are late. Carry an offline map, a headlamp, a whistle, water and clothing for changing weather. Stay with your group.

If you lose your way, stop and check the map and landmarks. Retrace your steps only if you know the route and it is safe. If you cannot do that, call emergency services. Share your coordinates if available, your last known location, nearby landmarks and any injuries.

After calling for rescue, stay put unless your life is in immediate danger. Protect yourself from the weather, save your energy and phone battery, and use a whistle or light to signal.
