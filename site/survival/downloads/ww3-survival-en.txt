Civilian guidance for armed conflict, evacuation, nuclear fallout, chemical emergencies and loss of essential services.

In immediate danger?
Follow instructions from local authorities. Call your local emergency number as soon as it is safe to do so.

1. Air raids and shelling

When an air-raid warning sounds, move promptly to the nearest designated shelter and follow local instructions. Find the shelters near home, work and school ahead of time, including one everyone in your household can reach. Enable official alerts and keep a battery radio for outages.

If you cannot reach a shelter safely, get inside a sturdy, undamaged building. Move away from windows, balconies and glass doors to an interior room. If shelling catches you outdoors and you cannot reach shelter, get low and cover your head.

Stay sheltered until officials give the all-clear, even if the noise stops. If fire or another immediate danger makes your shelter unsafe, use a safe exit if possible and seek help from emergency services.

2. Sheltering or evacuating

Read or listen to the full alert so you know which area it applies to and whether to shelter or leave. When officials tell your area to evacuate, leave promptly using the designated route or transport. Avoid shortcuts and obey closures. Return only when authorities say it is safe.

Decide in advance where you would go and how you would get there. Ask emergency management about accessible transport and shelters if you need them. Include children, carers, service animals and pets in your plan, and confirm that your destination can meet their needs.

If they are ready to grab, take identification, essential medicines, a phone and charger, and mobility or communication aids. Tell a trusted contact where you are going when you can do so safely.

3. Keeping families together

Choose a meeting place and a trusted contact outside the area before a crisis. Put their details on a card and carry it. If separated and networks are busy, try a short text. Agree with the school or care service on how to reunite safely. Help children learn their name and a caregiver’s phone number when they are old enough.

If someone is missing after a conflict-related evacuation, report it through local authorities or the Red Cross or Red Crescent family tracing service. If you find a child alone, stay with them in a safe place and contact the responsible authorities. Share the child’s personal details privately with them and keep those details off social media.

4. Mines and unexploded ordnance

Avoid closed or marked areas and use routes designated by local authorities. If you see suspicious debris, a possible mine or unexploded ammunition, stop. Keep away. Leave it untouched and warn others to stay clear. If you have a known safe way back, carefully withdraw that way. If you suspect you are in a mined area and cannot reach safe ground without stepping onto unknown ground, stay still and call for help.

Report what you saw and its location to local emergency services from safety if possible. Never return to take photos or collect objects. If someone is hurt in a suspected mined area, call specialist responders immediately and stay out of the area.

5. Nuclear explosion and the first 24 hours

If you see the flash of a suspected nuclear blast, look away immediately. Drop face down and protect your eyes. Stay down until the blast waves pass. As soon as you can move safely, go inside the nearest building. A sturdy brick or concrete building is preferable if you can reach it quickly.

Go to the basement or the middle of the building, away from windows, outside walls and the roof. Close doors and windows. If possible, switch off ventilation that draws in outside air. A car gives little protection from radioactive fallout. Bring pets in with you if you can do so safely.

Plan to stay in shelter for at least the first 24 hours unless officials give other instructions or there is an immediate threat such as fire or collapse. Listen for local updates. If the shelter becomes unsafe, move to another safe building. After 24 hours, keep following instructions about when and where to leave.

6. Remove radioactive dust

Get indoors quickly. If you were outside, carefully remove your outer layer of clothing without shaking it. Seal it in a bag or other container away from people and pets. Take a lukewarm shower with soap and shampoo. Skip hair conditioner, wash gently and keep cuts covered. Put on clean clothing stored indoors.

If you cannot shower, wash exposed skin at a tap or wipe it with a clean damp cloth, focusing on your face and hands. Seal used wipes or cloths with the clothing. If a pet was outside, gently wash it with soap or pet shampoo, rinse thoroughly and wash your hands and face afterward. Use waterproof gloves if you have them.

7. Food, water and iodine after a radiation emergency

Use sealed bottled drinks and packaged food, or unspoiled food already stored inside away from fallout. Wipe potentially dusty packaging with a damp cloth before opening it. Seal the cloth away from people and pets, then wash your hands. Wait for officials to clear garden produce and local water sources. Boiling water leaves radioactive material behind. Tap water can generally still be used for washing in a radiation emergency. Follow any separate restrictions from your water supplier.

Take potassium iodide (KI) only if public-health authorities instruct you to, and follow their directions for timing and dose. It helps protect only the thyroid from radioactive iodine. Keep sheltering as directed. Iodized salt and iodine supplements cannot replace KI tablets.

8. Chemical release

Move away from the release. If the chemical was released indoors and you were exposed, go outside to fresh air. For an outdoor release, follow local alerts about sheltering or evacuating. If told to shelter, close windows and doors and turn off the ventilation that brings in outside air. Use the room and sealing measures officials direct.

After contact with the chemical, remove contaminated clothing promptly and shower with lukewarm water and mild soap if you can, ideally within 10 minutes. Wash gently. If you cannot shower, blot the chemical off without rubbing. Keep contaminated items sealed away from others and follow disposal directions. If your eyes burn or your vision changes, rinse your eyes with clean water for 10 to 15 minutes.

Call emergency services for difficulty breathing or other severe symptoms. Contact your local poison centre or a healthcare provider for exposure advice. If a chemical was swallowed, do not induce vomiting or drink anything unless a professional tells you to.

9. Communication outages and safe return

Keep a battery-powered radio, spare batteries, a charged power bank and a paper list of essential numbers. Note which local station and official channels carry alerts. If mobile service is overloaded, send a short text with your location, status and needs to your household contact. Use emergency lines for urgent help. Check the time, area and issuer on alerts before acting on forwarded messages.

After a radiation release, use the routes and reception sites named by officials when they direct you to leave. Return home only after authorities say the area is safe. Keep away from visibly damaged buildings and fallen power lines. If you smell gas, leave without using switches or flames and contact emergency services from a safe place.

10. Household plan and three-day kit

Write down your emergency number, official alert channels, an out-of-area contact and two meeting places: one near home and one outside the neighbourhood. Work out how children will be collected and how anyone who needs accessible transport, backup power or daily care will get help. Keep paper copies and practise the plan.

Store at least three days of essentials at home. Plan for roughly four litres of water per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene, with extra for pets, heat and medical needs. Stock shelf-stable food, a manual can opener, a torch, radio, spare batteries, power bank, hygiene supplies and a first-aid kit.

Pack a bag you can carry with water, snacks, medication, essential documents, cash, warm layers and a phone charger. Ask a pharmacist about extra medicine and storage. Check supplies and batteries twice a year.

11. Safe water and sanitation

Check the exact water advisory. If officials say not to drink or use tap water, follow the limits they give and use a safe supply instead. Use safe water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, ice and infant formula.

For a boil-water advisory, use sealed bottled water or bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. Above about 2,000 metres, boil it for three minutes. Let it cool in a clean, covered container. Boiling, household filters and disinfectants cannot make water contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive material safe.

Wash your hands with soap and safe water, especially after using the toilet and before handling food. If water and soap are unavailable, use hand sanitiser containing at least 60% alcohol.

12. First aid while waiting for help

Check for dangers such as traffic, fire and live wires before approaching. For a life-threatening emergency, call the local emergency number, give your exact location and follow the dispatcher’s instructions. If someone is unresponsive and is not breathing normally or is only gasping, start CPR and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) as soon as it is available. Follow its prompts.

For heavy external bleeding, press firmly and continuously on the wound with gauze or a clean cloth. If an object is embedded, leave it in place and press around it.

Cool a heat burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not use ice or pull away anything stuck to the burn. Seek urgent help for large, deep, electrical or chemical burns. Learn first aid and CPR in a practical course before an emergency.

13. Power outages and food safety

Use a torch or battery lantern. Run generators only outdoors at least six metres from doors, windows and vents, with the exhaust pointed away from buildings. Never use a gas oven, grill or camping stove indoors for heat. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get outside into fresh air and call emergency services.

Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. An unopened fridge keeps perishables cold for about four hours. A closed full freezer lasts about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24 hours. After four hours without power or another cold source, discard refrigerated perishables. Never taste food to check its safety.

If you depend on powered medical equipment or refrigerated medicine, ask your provider in advance about safe backup power, storage and when to relocate.
